Derivation of the Effective Acceleration Equation in a Rotating Reference Frame

To derive the expression for the effective acceleration $\vec{a}_{\text{eff}}$ observed in a rotating reference frame ($\mathcal{R}$), taking into account the fictitious forces (centrifugal, Coriolis, and Euler accelerations) that arise purely from the rotation of the coordinate system.

Step 1

1. Basic Concepts & The Transport Theorem

We consider two frames of reference sharing a common origin:

  • Inertial Frame ($\mathcal{I}$): A frame that is not accelerating or rotating, where Newton's laws hold in their simplest form ($\vec{F} = m\vec{a}_{\mathcal{I}}$).
  • Rotating Frame ($\mathcal{R}$): A frame that rotates with an angular velocity vector $\vec{\omega}(t)$ relative to the inertial frame.

Because the basis unit vectors $(\hat{e}_1, \hat{e}_2, \hat{e}_3)$ of $\mathcal{R}$ rotate at rate $\frac{d\hat{e}_i}{dt} = \vec{\omega} \times \hat{e}_i$, for any differentiable vector $\vec{A}$, the relationship between its time derivative in the inertial and rotating frames (the Transport Theorem) is given by:

$$ \left( \frac{d\vec{A}}{dt} \right)_{\mathcal{I}} = \left( \frac{d\vec{A}}{dt} \right)_{\mathcal{R}} + \vec{\omega} \times \vec{A} $$
$\left( \frac{d\vec{A}}{dt} \right)_{\mathcal{I}}$
Time rate of change measured by a stationary inertial observer.
$\left( \frac{d\vec{A}}{dt} \right)_{\mathcal{R}}$
Time rate of change of components measured inside the rotating frame.
$\vec{\omega} \times \vec{A}$
Apparent change caused by the rotation of the coordinate axes.
Step 2

2. Position and Velocity Vectors

Let $\vec{r}$ be the position vector of the particle in both frames (assuming their origins coincide):

Velocity in Inertial Frame ($\vec{v}_{\mathcal{I}}$)
$$ \vec{v}_{\mathcal{I}} = \left( \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} \right)_{\mathcal{I}} $$
Velocity in Rotating Frame ($\vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}$)
$$ \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}} = \left( \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} \right)_{\mathcal{R}} $$

Applying the Transport Theorem from Step 1 to the position vector $\vec{A} = \vec{r}$:

$$ \vec{v}_{\mathcal{I}} = \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}} + \vec{\omega} \times \vec{r} $$
Step 3 Differentiating Velocity in $\mathcal{I}$

3. Acceleration in the Inertial Frame

The true acceleration in the inertial frame is the inertial time derivative of $\vec{v}_{\mathcal{I}}$:

$$ \vec{a}_{\mathcal{I}} = \left( \frac{d\vec{v}_{\mathcal{I}}}{dt} \right)_{\mathcal{I}} $$

To express $\vec{a}_{\mathcal{I}}$ in terms of quantities measured in the rotating frame, we substitute $\vec{v}_{\mathcal{I}} = \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}} + \vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}$ and differentiate with respect to time in $\mathcal{I}$:

$$ \vec{a}_{\mathcal{I}} = \left( \frac{d}{dt} \left( \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}} + \vec{\omega} \times \vec{r} \right) \right)_{\mathcal{I}} $$

By linearity, we apply the inertial time derivative operator to both terms separately:

$$ \vec{a}_{\mathcal{I}} = \underbrace{\left( \frac{d\vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}}{dt} \right)_{\mathcal{I}}}_{\text{First Term}} + \underbrace{\left( \frac{d}{dt} \left( \vec{\omega} \times \vec{r} \right) \right)_{\mathcal{I}}}_{\text{Second Term}} $$
Step 4

4. Expanding Each Term

First Term (Applying the Transport Theorem to $\vec{A} = \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}$):

$$ \left( \frac{d\vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}}{dt} \right)_{\mathcal{I}} = \left( \frac{d\vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}}{dt} \right)_{\mathcal{R}} + \vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}} = \vec{a}_{\mathcal{R}} + \vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}} $$

Second Term (Product rule on $\vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}$):

$$ \left( \frac{d}{dt} \left( \vec{\omega} \times \vec{r} \right) \right)_{\mathcal{I}} = \frac{d\vec{\omega}}{dt} \times \vec{r} + \vec{\omega} \times \left( \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} \right)_{\mathcal{I}} $$

Since $\left(\frac{d\vec{r}}{dt}\right)_{\mathcal{I}} = \vec{v}_{\mathcal{I}} = \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}} + \vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}$, substituting this into $\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{I}}$ yields:

$$ \vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{I}} = \vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}} + \vec{\omega} \times (\vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}) $$
Why is there a factor of $2$ in $-2\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}$?
Notice that $\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}$ arises from two distinct physical effects of equal magnitude:
  1. First Term: The velocity vector $\vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}$ is measured relative to rotating axes whose directions change in time ($\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}$).
  2. Second Term: As the particle moves radially/tangentially with velocity $\vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}$, the frame's sweep velocity $\vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}$ changes at rate $\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}$.
Step 5 Combining All 4 Contributions

5. Combining the Terms

Summing the expanded First and Second terms together, the two $\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}$ terms combine into $2\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}$:

$$ \vec{a}_{\mathcal{I}} = \vec{a}_{\mathcal{R}} + 2\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}} + \frac{d\vec{\omega}}{dt} \times \vec{r} + \vec{\omega} \times (\vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}) $$
Step 6

6. Final Form of the Effective Acceleration

To find the acceleration $\vec{a}_{\mathcal{R}}$ as observed inside the rotating frame, we solve the equation in Step 5 for $\vec{a}_{\mathcal{R}}$:

$$ \vec{a}_{\mathcal{R}} = \vec{a}_{\mathcal{I}} - \vec{\omega} \times (\vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}) - 2\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}} - \frac{d\vec{\omega}}{dt} \times \vec{r} $$

Thus, defining $\vec{a}_{\text{eff}} \equiv \vec{a}_{\mathcal{R}}$, the effective acceleration in the rotating frame is:

$$ \vec{a}_{\text{eff}} = \vec{a}_{\mathcal{I}} - \vec{\omega} \times (\vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}) - 2\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}} - \frac{d\vec{\omega}}{dt} \times \vec{r} $$

7. Physical Interpretation of Fictitious Terms

● Centrifugal Acceleration: $-\vec{\omega} \times (\vec{\omega} \times \vec{r})$ Click to Simulate →

Always acts radially outward perpendicular to the axis of rotation with magnitude $\omega^2 r_{\perp}$. It depends only on position $\vec{r}$ and angular speed $|\vec{\omega}|$, even when the particle is stationary in $\mathcal{R}$.

● Coriolis Acceleration: $-2\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}$ Click to Simulate →

Acts perpendicular to both $\vec{\omega}$ and the rotating velocity $\vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}$. In a counter-clockwise rotating frame ($\omega_z > 0$), it deflects moving objects to the right of their direction of travel, doing zero work since $\vec{a}_{\text{cor}} \perp \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}$.

● Euler (Azimuthal) Acceleration: $-\frac{d\vec{\omega}}{dt} \times \vec{r}$ Click to Simulate →

Occurs only when the angular velocity $\vec{\omega}(t)$ is changing over time ($d\vec{\omega}/dt \neq 0$). It pushes tangentially opposite to the angular acceleration — the backward jerk you feel when a carousel suddenly starts spinning up.

Step 8 Summary

8. Summary

The complete equation of motion in a rotating reference frame replaces the inertial acceleration with the effective acceleration:

$$ \vec{a}_{\text{eff}} = \vec{a}_{\mathcal{I}} \color{#ff5555}{-\vec{\omega} \times (\vec{\omega} \times \vec{r})} \color{#50fa7b}{- 2\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\mathcal{R}}} \color{#ffb86c}{- \frac{d\vec{\omega}}{dt} \times \vec{r}} $$
  • $\vec{a}_{\mathcal{I}} = \vec{F}_{\text{real}}/m$ is the true inertial acceleration caused by physical interactions.
  • The three subtracted terms represent the centrifugal, Coriolis, and Euler accelerations that account for the non-inertial nature of the rotating coordinate system $\mathcal{R}$.
